# Ezekiel Introduction — Covenant, Exile, and God’s Glory

## 4-Sentence Summary
Ezekiel is a prophetic book set in exile, where God speaks to a covenant-breaking people through a priest-prophet who watches over the covenant.
The book is often difficult because it is long, repetitive, and contains severe judgment, but its main message is that God is holy, faithful, and still at work in judgment and restoration.
Ezekiel fits into the larger Bible story of covenant, especially the consequences of covenant unfaithfulness and the hope of renewal.
Its imagery and themes deeply shape later Scripture, especially Revelation and the teaching of Jesus.

## Main Themes

### 1. Ezekiel’s Setting
- Ezekiel receives the word of the Lord in the land of the Chaldeans by the river Chebar during the exile of Judah.
- The opening vision marks Ezekiel as a prophet who speaks from the context of national collapse and displacement.
- Verse references: Ezekiel 1:1–3

### 2. Ezekiel as a Covenant Prophet
- Ezekiel is not just delivering information; he is acting like a covenant attorney.
- His role is to announce Israel’s covenant unfaithfulness and the consequences of breaking covenant with God.
- The repeated use of “Lord GOD” highlights God as both the covenant God and the personal Master of his people.
- Verse references: Ezekiel 2:3–7; Ezekiel 3:16–21; Ezekiel 5:5–17; Ezekiel 18:1–32

### 3. The Holiness of God
- Ezekiel must be read with the understanding that God is holy and cannot ignore sin.
- Much of the book shows that God’s judgment is not random or cruel, but a response to persistent covenant violation.
- Ezekiel’s priestly background matters because holiness, purity, and defilement are central to his ministry.
- Verse references: Ezekiel 1:4–28; Ezekiel 8:1–18; Ezekiel 10:18–19; Ezekiel 43:1–12

### 4. Judgment and Exile
- The exile fulfills covenant warnings: God scatters his people when they persist in disobedience.
- The book reflects the curses of covenant unfaithfulness, especially the loss of land, temple, and security.
- Ezekiel presents exile as both punishment and a stage in God’s larger redemptive purpose.
- Verse references: Deuteronomy 28:15–68; Ezekiel 12:8–16; Ezekiel 20:23–24; Ezekiel 22:15–16

### 5. Hope of Restoration
- Ezekiel does not end in judgment; it moves toward cleansing, renewal, new life, and restored worship.
- God promises a new heart, a new spirit, and life breathed into what was dead.
- The final vision presents renewed presence, land, and flowing life from God’s temple.
- Verse references: Ezekiel 36:24–28; Ezekiel 37:1–14; Ezekiel 40–48

## Big Picture of Covenant
- The Bible is best read as the story of covenant rather than just a collection of books.
- Key covenant stages mentioned in the transcript include:
  - Creation covenant — humanity created for relationship with God.
  - Noahic covenant — general grace and preservation of the world.
  - Abrahamic covenant — specific grace and promise.
  - Sinai covenant — holiness, law, and national identity.
  - Levitical covenant — priestly mediation and holiness.
  - Davidic covenant — kingdom and kingship.
  - New covenant — renewal, resurrection life, and transformation.
- Ezekiel stands within this covenant storyline and exposes what happens when covenant fidelity is broken.
- Verse references: Genesis 1–3; Genesis 9:8–17; Genesis 12:1–3; Exodus 19–24; 2 Samuel 7:12–16; Jeremiah 31:31–34; Ezekiel 36:26–27

## Key Connections to the New Testament
- Ezekiel 36:26–27 connects with the New Birth language in John 3:5.
- Ezekiel 34 shapes Jesus’ teaching about the Good Shepherd in John 10:11.
- Ezekiel 37 influences the picture of one people from Jews and Gentiles in Ephesians 2:14–18.
- Ezekiel 47 strongly echoes in the imagery of Revelation 22:1–2.
- The book’s visions and symbols also shape Revelation’s imagery of sealing, judgment, temple, and final restoration.
- Verse references: John 3:5; John 10:11; Ephesians 2:14–18; Revelation 7:1–4; Revelation 22:1–2

## Main Points to Remember
- Ezekiel is hard because it is long, intense, and culturally distant, but its message is coherent.
- The book is about God’s holiness, covenant unfaithfulness, judgment, and future restoration.
- Ezekiel must be read from the big picture before chasing details.
- The book shows that God is not finished with his people even in exile.

## Application
- Read Ezekiel looking for the big story, not just isolated strange visions.
- Let the book confront sin seriously, especially the danger of treating God casually.
- Remember that judgment in Ezekiel is meant to expose covenant failure and drive people back to God.
- Hold onto hope: God can give a new heart, restore worship, and bring life out of death.
- Apply the book personally by asking where your life needs covenant faithfulness, repentance, and renewed obedience.

## Study Notes
- Ezekiel was a priest-prophet, so holiness language is central.
- The opening vision establishes that God is still present even in exile.
- The repeated divine speech formula emphasizes that the message originates with God, not Ezekiel.
- The final chapters show that the last word is not destruction but restoration and divine presence.

## Simple Outline
1. Introduction and call of Ezekiel — Ezekiel 1–3
2. Judgment on Jerusalem and Judah — Ezekiel 4–24
3. Judgment on the nations — Ezekiel 25–32
4. Hope, restoration, and new life — Ezekiel 33–48
