# Ezra — Teaching Notes

## Main idea
God is faithful to His promises, but Israel’s restoration is still incomplete because sin remains a problem.

## Why this book matters
- Ezra is a post-exilic historical book.
- It helps explain what happens after exile.
- It connects the Old Testament story to the time before Jesus.
- It shows that restoration is not just about land or buildings, but about the people’s hearts.
- Even after return and reform, Israel still needs a greater solution.

## Post-exilic timeline
- 538 BC: Cyrus issues the decree.
- 536 BC: First return under Zerubbabel.
- 515 BC: Temple completed.
- 479 BC: Esther becomes queen.
- 458 BC: Ezra’s return.
- 444 BC: Nehemiah’s return.
- Around 430 BC: Later Nehemiah activity / end of the narrative period.

## Persian kings
- Cyrus
- Darius
- Xerxes / Ahasuerus
- Artaxerxes

## Books in this period
- Ezra: return, temple, Torah
- Nehemiah: wall, renewal, failure
- Esther: during Xerxes
- Haggai and Zechariah: temple rebuilding era
- Malachi: later in the post-exilic period

## Structure of Ezra
### Ezra 1–6: Temple
- Return begins.
- Temple rebuilding is delayed and then completed.

### Ezra 7–10: Torah / Law
- Ezra arrives.
- The Law is brought back into focus.
- Spiritual reform begins.

## Structure of Ezra-Nehemiah together
### Restoration
- Temple rebuilt
- Law restored
- Wall rebuilt

### Attempted renewal
- The people try to return to covenant faithfulness.

### Failure
- Sin returns.
- The ending is disappointing, not triumphant.

## Main point
External rebuilding does not solve internal sin.

## Authorship observations
- Ezra 7–10 shifts into first person, which suggests Ezra is personally involved.
- Ezra is called a skilled scribe.
- The book includes lists, records, and details that feel like careful historical compilation.
- Some early material describes events Ezra did not personally witness.
- Nehemiah also contains first-person sections, suggesting the final form may involve more than one contributor, though Ezra-Nehemiah was originally one work.

## Audience
- The book is aimed mainly at a Jewish audience.
- This is seen in lists of returnees, assumed knowledge of Jewish festivals and customs, and relevance to covenant identity and national history.
- It likely speaks to returned exiles and later Jewish generations.

## Dating
- The book covers the period from Cyrus to Artaxerxes.
- It must be written after the events it describes.
- The final compilation is likely after Nehemiah’s later activity.
- Ezra 4 is important because it is not in straight chronological order.
